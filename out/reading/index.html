Incident report: delayed order confirmations

A

This report describes an incident at Northstar Market, a fictional online shop. On Tuesday morning, several customers paid for orders but did not immediately receive confirmation emails. The website continued to accept orders, and payment processing remained available. At first, the support team thought the issue involved a few incorrect email addresses. However, similar complaints arrived from different customers within ten minutes. The incident affected confirmation messages, not the orders themselves. This distinction was important because customers needed to know whether they should try buying their items again.

B

At 09:12, a support agent posted three examples in the engineering channel. Each example included an order number and the approximate payment time, but no card information. Maya, the engineer on duty, checked the email queue and found that its oldest unfinished message was eighteen minutes old. The usual delay was less than one minute. She declared an incident at 09:18 and asked another engineer, Ben, to help investigate. Maya kept responsibility for coordinating the response so that Ben could focus on the technical problem without answering every new question.

C

The team had released a small change to the email worker at 08:55. The change added a delivery-status check before sending a message. During normal testing, that check returned quickly. In production, however, the external status service became slow. Each worker waited for a response before handling the next message, so unfinished work gradually filled the queue. The release did not include a limit on how long the worker could wait. The engineers found no evidence that the payment service or the order database was involved in the delay.

D

At 09:26, Ben suggested rolling back the worker to its previous version. Maya agreed after checking that the old version could safely read the messages already in the queue. The rollback finished at 09:31. New confirmation messages then began moving through the system, but the team did not immediately close the incident. They watched both new orders and older messages because a successful deployment alone would not prove recovery. By 09:47, the oldest message was less than one minute old again, and a test order had received its confirmation.

E

Support sent affected customers a short explanation while engineers monitored recovery. The message said that their orders were recorded and that they should avoid placing the same order again. It also explained that confirmation emails would arrive later than usual. Support did not promise a specific delivery time for every email because some messages were still waiting. Customers who had already placed duplicate orders were asked to contact support for help. The team used order numbers to investigate these cases instead of asking customers to share payment details in the public support channel.

F

The incident delayed 246 confirmation emails. No orders were lost, and the team found no duplicate charges caused by the email worker. Some customers contacted support more than once, which increased the workload for the morning shift. The report does not treat the absence of lost orders as a reason to dismiss the incident. A missing confirmation can make a completed purchase feel uncertain. It can also lead customers to repeat an action that has already succeeded. For that reason, the customer impact included confusion and additional effort as well as the measurable delay.

G

The review identified two gaps in the team's checks. First, the release tests covered successful responses but not a slow external service. Second, the existing alert measured the number of failed emails, while these messages were waiting rather than failing. The team will add a timeout and a test that simulates a slow response. An alert will also watch the age of the oldest queued message. These changes address separate problems: the timeout limits waiting, and the alert helps an engineer notice a growing delay before many customers report it.

H

Maya will own the new queue-age alert, and Ben will own the timeout and its tests. Both changes are due before the next worker release. The review deliberately focuses on system conditions rather than blaming the person who approved the change. The approval followed the checks available at the time, but those checks were incomplete. At the next engineering meeting, the team will review whether other background workers make similar calls to external services. The incident is considered resolved, while the prevention tasks remain open and will be tracked separately.
